A Definitional Crisis  ·  2025

What is AI?
It depends who you ask.

The word "artificial intelligence" means something genuinely different to every institution that uses it. This isn't a communications problem. It's a power problem — whoever controls the definition controls the conversation.

Nine perspectives  ·  One word  ·  Zero consensus
01 Academia

A field of inquiry, not a thing

Researchers define AI as the scientific study of computational systems that exhibit intelligent behaviour — not a product, not a feature, and certainly not any single tool. Within that discipline are machine learning, NLP, computer vision, robotics, and reinforcement learning, each with its own methods and distinct failure modes.

"Calling ChatGPT 'AI' is a bit like calling a hammer 'physics.' Not wrong, exactly. But it collapses a century of distinct inquiry into a brand name."

Why it matters: without this precision, we can't distinguish systems with very different risks.

02 Tech industry

Any intelligent feature in a product

For companies like Salesforce, Adobe, and Microsoft, "AI" is a feature label. It covers autocomplete, recommendation engines, search ranking, smart scheduling, and generative tools — all under the same badge. The definition is elastic by design: it signals modernity, not any specific technical capability.

"If you want to sell something to people today, you call it AI. So everything becomes AI and then people don't understand." — Yuval Noah Harari, 2024

Why it matters: it makes "AI" nearly impossible for consumers to evaluate meaningfully.

03 Journalism

A threat, a tool, and a story — simultaneously

Journalists cover AI through three simultaneous frames: as a technological capability, as an economic disruptor, and as a cultural force. The definition shifts depending on which story is being told — so the same system can be saviour and villain in the same news cycle, sometimes in the same publication.

"The problem with covering AI is that every story is accurate and incomplete simultaneously. The technology is genuinely multiple things at once."

Why it matters: journalistic framing shapes public perception more than any other single source.

04 Regulators & Law

A system that produces consequential outputs

The EU AI Act defines AI as machine-based systems that generate predictions, recommendations, decisions, or content. The US approach focuses on risk tiers. Both definitions are deliberately functional — what matters legally is what the system does to people, not how it works internally or what its creators call it.

"For regulatory purposes, the mechanism is less important than the impact. A system that affects a hiring decision is AI regardless of its architecture."

Why it matters: this catches systems companies wouldn't voluntarily call AI — fraud filters, credit scoring, content moderation.

05 Finance

An efficiency multiplier and a liability variable

Banks, insurers, and asset managers define AI operationally: it is whatever automates a previously human judgment at scale. The financial sector has used algorithmic decision-making since the 1980s. What's new is the opacity — modern AI makes credit, fraud, and trading decisions in ways even operators can't fully explain after the fact.

"AI in financial services isn't a technology story. It's a liability story. The question isn't what it can do. It's who's responsible when it goes wrong."

Why it matters: the finance definition is the most grounded — and the most alert to failure modes.

06 General public

Robots, voice assistants, and the end of jobs

Public understanding of AI is built from decades of science fiction, news headlines, and product marketing — producing a picture that blends Terminator-style autonomy with the mundane reality of Siri and Netflix recommendations. Most people can't define it precisely, but almost everyone has a feeling about it.

"The public doesn't need a technical definition. They need to know: can it be trusted? Who is responsible? And will it cost me my job?"

Why it matters: public perception drives political will — and political will shapes how regulation actually unfolds.

07 Government & Policy

A strategic asset and a national security concern

Governments define AI as a domain of geopolitical competition — a set of capabilities that determine future economic and military power. The definitions produced by bodies like NIST focus on safety, risk, and accountability, but the underlying motivation is competitiveness. Every major AI policy is ultimately a statement about not falling behind.

"Every major government document on AI is ultimately about two things: not losing the race to China, and not getting blamed when something goes wrong domestically."

Why it matters: policy definitions determine which systems get scrutinised and which are quietly left alone.

08 Military & Defence

Autonomous decision-making at speed and scale

Defence establishments define AI primarily around speed and autonomy — systems that can perceive, decide, and act faster than human operators allow. The central concern isn't intelligence in a philosophical sense, but reliability under adversarial conditions and the deeply contested question of human oversight in lethal decisions.

"The military definition of AI is not about making machines smarter. It's about removing the human bottleneck from decisions that need to happen in milliseconds."

Why it matters: this is where the ethics of human-in-the-loop are most consequential — and least settled.

09 Philosophy

A mirror pointed at human intelligence

Philosophers are less interested in what AI can do and more interested in what its existence forces us to confront: what counts as thinking? Can a system understand, or only imitate understanding? The field has never settled on a definition of intelligence itself, which makes defining "artificial intelligence" a recursively difficult problem with no clean answer.

"We built a machine that appears to understand language. Now we have to figure out whether 'appears to' and 'does' are actually different — and it turns out that question is very hard."

Why it matters: the definitions we choose encode assumptions about consciousness, agency, and moral status.

The core problem

These nine definitions are not competing versions of the same truth. They reflect genuinely different interests, frameworks, and stakes. A regulator and a product marketer using the word "AI" in the same conversation are not speaking the same language — and the consequences compound with every decision made on that basis. Until we develop stable, shared vocabulary for what these systems actually are, the confusion is not a bug in how people think about AI. It is a structural feature of the conversation itself.
