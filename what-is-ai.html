The word "artificial intelligence" means something genuinely different to every institution that uses it. An AI company, a regulator, a bank, a journalist, a researcher, and a product manager are not speaking the same language when they say "AI" — and the confusion isn't accidental. Whoever controls the definition controls the conversation.

This matters practically. When a CEO tells shareholders the company is "deploying AI," when a senator asks a tech executive whether AI is safe, when your employer says they're "integrating AI into workflows" — these statements carry entirely different meanings depending on whose definition is operating. You can't evaluate any of them without knowing which frame is being used.

The six definitions

Here is what six different kinds of institutions actually mean when they say AI, and why each definition serves their interests.

AI companies

OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind define AI as systems capable of learning from data and improving over time. The emphasis is on autonomy and general capability — AI doesn't just follow rules, it generates novel outputs. The definition expands with every product launch, because a broader definition justifies broader valuations.

Regulators

The EU AI Act defines AI as "machine-based systems that can generate outputs such as predictions, recommendations, decisions or content." The US approach focuses on risk tiers. Both definitions are deliberately functional — what matters is what the system does to people, not how it works internally. This catches systems companies wouldn't voluntarily call AI: fraud filters, credit scoring, content moderation.

For regulatory purposes, the mechanism is less important than the impact. A system that affects a hiring decision is AI regardless of its architecture.

The financial sector

Banks and asset managers define AI operationally: it is whatever automates a previously human judgment at scale. The financial sector has used algorithmic decision-making since the 1980s. What's new is the opacity — modern AI makes credit, fraud, and trading decisions in ways even operators can't fully explain. Their definition is the most grounded, and the most alert to failure modes.

Why none of this is an accident

These six definitions are not competing versions of the same truth. They reflect genuinely different interests, frameworks, and stakes. A regulator and a product marketer using the word "AI" in the same conversation are not speaking the same language. The consequences compound with every decision made on that basis.

The practical upshot: when someone tells you something about AI — a capability, a risk, a promise — the first question to ask is which definition they're operating from. That single question will tell you more about what they actually mean than anything else they say.
