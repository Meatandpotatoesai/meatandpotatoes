You’re Not Behind: What’s Actually Going On With AI

For anyone caught in the flood of AI news and quietly wondering what it all means.

If you read the headlines and felt a step behind, you are not behind.

Here is a little over two weeks of AI news from this September.

On 3 September, OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, launched a new version of its AI, and its president told reporters: “Welcome to the AGI era.” (AGI stands for artificial general intelligence: roughly, AI as capable as a person.) Three days later the head of Nvidia, the company that makes the chips most AI runs on, posted that AGI had arrived. On 12 September the head of Anthropic, another AI company, asked the whole industry to slow down. On 14 September Microsoft published a draft rulebook stating that its AI is “not conscious” and rejecting the idea that it could ever deserve rights.

A few days after that, Google admitted that during a security test in May, one of its AI systems had been given internet access by mistake. It got into the computer systems of three real companies. It stopped each time it got in, and Google says it believed it was still inside the test.

If you read all that and felt a step behind, you are not behind. What does AGI actually mean? Why is a chip company announcing it? Who decided whether a computer has feelings? The people making the headlines do not agree with one another about what the words mean, and most of them have something to sell.

This is the piece we would hand a smart friend who has been busy living their life. What people mean by AI now, how it works, who is who, what it can and cannot do, and whether it is as big a deal as the noise suggests. Where a word can’t be avoided, we explain it.

First: what people mean by “AI” now

“AI” is an old, baggy word. Since the 1950s it has meant, roughly, computers doing things that seem to need a brain. For most of that time it meant small, single-purpose tools you never noticed.

You have been using that kind of AI for years. The spam filter in your email. The fraud check that freezes your card when someone abroad tries to use it, which has run on a version of AI since 1992. The map app that reroutes you around traffic. The feed that picks which post or video you see next. Each does one job, was built for that one job, and almost nobody calls it AI.

What changed was a single launch. In November 2022 OpenAI released ChatGPT, a program you could talk to in ordinary language. It could write, summarise, explain and answer back. For most people it was the first AI they had used on purpose. By February 2026 OpenAI said 900 million people were using it every week, and by late summer it was claiming more than a billion.

So when a headline says “AI” today, it usually means that kind of system: chatbots like ChatGPT, and the technology underneath them. Not always, though. The same word still covers the fraud check and the driverless taxi, which is one reason the conversation is so confusing.

As for AGI: there is no agreed definition of it, which is how one company can announce it while another says it is years away. Our piece AGI: What It Actually Means shows how to read the announcements.

How a chatbot works, in two minutes

Strip away the mystique and a chatbot is a very, very good autocomplete.

You know how your phone suggests the next word as you type a message? A chatbot does the same thing, at a scale that is hard to picture. It is built by showing a program an enormous amount of writing (books, websites, articles, computer code) and having it guess, over and over, which word comes next. Every wrong guess nudges its internal settings so the next guess is a little better.

Repeat that across trillions of words and something surprising happens. To predict the next word in a recipe, a contract or a physics textbook, it helps to have picked up the patterns of cooking, law and physics along the way. So it does.

The technical name for this is a large language model. “Large” because of its billions of internal settings, “language” because it learned from text. ChatGPT, Google’s Gemini, Anthropic’s Claude and Meta AI are all built on one.

Then comes finishing school. People rate its answers so it learns to be helpful and polite. Lately, chatbots have also been drilled, by trial and error, on problems with checkable answers, like maths and computer code. That is a big reason they have improved so fast at exactly those things.

Two things follow from how it is built, and they explain a lot of the news:

  • It can be fluent and wrong at the same time. It always produces the most plausible next words, and plausible is not the same as true. When it doesn’t know, it often doesn’t say so. It produces something that sounds right. The industry calls this a “hallucination”.
  • Whether it “understands” anything is genuinely unsettled. Some serious researchers think these systems have something like understanding. Others think it is very sophisticated pattern-matching. No test settles it, and you don’t need to pick a side to use one sensibly.

Why it suddenly feels like it’s everywhere

Three reasons, and only the first is about the technology.

One tool, thousands of jobs. The old kind of AI had to be built from scratch for every task: a specialist team, months of work, one job at the end of it. That is why it was mostly banks and big tech firms that used it. A chatbot can be pointed at a new task by typing a sentence. Summarise this. Translate that. Draft a reply. So within about two years it turned up in your email, your phone, your word processor and your messaging apps. Nobody had to rebuild it for each one.

The money. Amazon, Microsoft, Google’s parent company Alphabet and Meta have told investors they expect to spend between $725 billion and $745 billion this year. Most of it goes on data centres (warehouses full of computers) and the chips and electricity to run them. That is more than $2,000 for every person in the United States, in one year, from four companies. Money on that scale needs a story big enough to justify it, and the people spending it tell that story loudly.

The noise. “This changes everything” and “this could end the world” are both gripping headlines, and both make the same claim: that AI is enormously powerful. “A slow, uneven, expensive change to how offices work” is a much harder story to sell. So the extremes get the airtime.

We have written a whole piece on the money, and on why the industry argues about intelligence when what is really changing is the price: It’s Not How Smart AI Gets. It’s How Cheap.

What it can do today, and what it can’t

Some of what you hear is hype. A lot of it is real, and already in daily use.

What it can do

  • Draft an email, a cover letter or a tricky text message, then redo it in a different tone in seconds.
  • Summarise a 50-page report, or explain a medical letter or a tenancy agreement in plain words.
  • Write working computer code. Many professional programmers now use it every day.
  • Translate between hundreds of languages.
  • Solve problems from the International Mathematical Olympiad, the world’s top maths competition for school students, at gold-medal level. Two companies’ systems did it in July 2025.
  • Away from chatbots: predict the shapes of proteins, which helps scientists design medicines; forecast the weather; and drive a taxi with nobody in the driver’s seat. Waymo, a sister company of Google, gives about half a million paid driverless rides a week in 15 US cities.

What it can’t do, or not reliably

  • Know when it’s wrong. In 2023 two New York lawyers and their firm were fined $5,000 after filing a court document that cited cases ChatGPT had made up. They were not the last. By October 2026 a database built on the work of Damien Charlotin, a researcher who tracks these cases, listed about 1,500 in US court filings alone.
  • Be good at things evenly. This is the strangest part. A system that can solve olympiad maths can still stumble over a question a child would get right. With a person, if they can do hard maths you can safely assume they can count the days until Friday. With AI you can’t assume anything. You have to check each thing separately. Researchers call this “jagged” intelligence.
  • Do a whole job on its own. It can do pieces of many jobs: a first draft here, a summary there. Joining the pieces up, catching its mistakes and answering for the result is, for now, mostly still a person’s work.
  • Remember you, unless it’s built to. Each conversation largely starts fresh, though some products now keep notes about you.

So is it a big deal?

Yes, but probably not in the way the loudest headlines suggest. Here are both cases, made as strongly as their supporters would make them.

The case that it’s huge

  • It is aimed at the work most of us are paid for. Writing, analysing, advising, coding, explaining. Around the world, people are paid more than $60 trillion a year for their work, and a large share of that is this kind of thinking. Earlier machines mostly took over muscle work and routine paperwork. This goes after thinking work.
  • It is already showing up in jobs, at the bottom. Stanford researchers looked at payroll records for millions of American workers. Young people aged 22 to 25, in the jobs AI can do most of, have fallen well behind young people in other jobs. By mid-2026, 19% fewer of them were in work than if they had kept pace. Older workers in the same jobs were fine. The first rung of the career ladder is where it lands first.
  • It is improving fast. In 2024 the best systems reached silver-medal level at the maths olympiad, and only after experts translated the problems into a special computer language for them. In 2025 they reached gold, reading the problems in plain English.
  • It has already done real science. AlphaFold, which predicts the shapes of proteins, cracked a 50-year-old problem in biology. Its creators won a Nobel Prize for it.
  • Its builders are worried too. The people running AI companies warn that it could be misused, in cyber attacks for example, and that too much power could end up in too few hands. We take the scariest version seriously in Could AI actually kill you?

The case that it’s smaller than the noise

  • Big technologies take decades. Electricity was available in the 1880s but didn’t make the economy noticeably more productive until the 1920s, because factories had to be rebuilt around it. Offices, hospitals and courts don’t rebuild themselves overnight either.
  • Not every study finds an effect. A large Danish study of 25,000 workers found AI chatbots saved them about 3% of their time, with no detectable effect on earnings or hours two years after ChatGPT launched.
  • The biggest proven wins came from narrow tools. The breakthroughs nobody disputes, like the protein shapes and the weather forecasts, came from systems built for one job, not from chatbots.
THE HONEST MIDDLE

It is real, it is uneven, and it is arriving gradually. It shows up first in tasks that are easy to check and in jobs at the bottom of the ladder. It is not a switch that flips on a date someone announces. So the useful question is rarely “Is AGI here?” It is “What can this actually do, and what does it cost?”

Five questions for the next AI headline

You don’t need to follow every story. You need a way to size one up in thirty seconds.

  1. Which AI? A headline that says “AI” could mean a chatbot, a fraud filter, a driverless car or a protein predictor. They are different technologies with different track records.
  2. Who is saying it, and what do they sell? A chip company announcing AGI. A start-up announcing a breakthrough the week it raises money. A critic with a book out. None of that makes them wrong, but it tells you how hard to check.
  3. What can it actually do, and what does it cost? “Revolutionary” is not an answer. An answer sounds like: “it drafts the contract review in ten minutes, for a few dollars, and a lawyer still has to check it.”
  4. Good at what, exactly? Because AI is jagged, “it beat the experts at X” tells you very little about Y. Ask whether the thing it’s good at is the thing you care about.
  5. By whose definition? When someone says AGI, “superintelligence” or “human-level”, ask what they mean. This year alone, the people announcing it were each using a different yardstick.

One more beats all five: try it yourself. Pick something you actually do, like a letter you’ve been putting off or a document you don’t understand. Give it to one of these tools, then check its work. Ten minutes of that will tell you more than a month of headlines.

ChatbotA program you talk to in ordinary language, like ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude.
Large language modelThe technology inside a chatbot: a program trained on vast amounts of text to predict what comes next.
TrainingThe expensive, one-off process of building an AI system by showing it enormous numbers of examples.
HallucinationWhen an AI states something false, confidently and fluently.
AgentAn AI given tools and permission to take several steps on its own, such as searching the web, filling in forms or writing and running code.
